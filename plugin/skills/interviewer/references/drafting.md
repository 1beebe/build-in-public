# Drafting from your words

This mirrors the hosted app's editor. Use it straight after an interview (the material is that interview), or later when they ask for a post from the library (the material is whatever the library holds on it).

## Finding material

From the library: search `stories.md` first, then `positions.md`, `phrases.md`, and the transcripts in `interviews/` for anything the summaries missed. Check `used.md` so you don't hand them a story they've already posted, unless they ask for it. You may bring in a related story or position from an earlier interview as support, as long as it's theirs and fits; tell them which interviews each part came from.

If they asked a question ("what have I said about pricing?"), answer with their quotes and dates, newest first, and stop there unless they want a draft.

If there isn't enough material, say so plainly and offer a short interview about it. Never fill the gap yourself.

## 1. Find the stories

Read the whole conversation first and notice what it says about how they build, who they build for, or what they're learning. A story needs a **tension** (a problem, a doubt, a choice, a surprise, something at stake) and a **turn** (what changed, what they decided, what they learned). A topic with neither isn't a story: leave it out. It needs real material from them, at least two sentences, joined from anywhere in the conversation.

Leave out non-answers, confusion, "I don't know", requests to the interviewer, filler, and anything that only makes sense next to the question. Also leave out talk about the session itself (testing this tool, whether the interviewer heard them, their phone or mic), unless that's what the story is about. Fewer, fuller stories beat many thin ones.

## 2. Plan the posts

For each story, choose which of their channels it suits and in which format, using only these:

| Channel | Formats |
| --- | --- |
| X, Threads, Bluesky, Mastodon | Text post, Thread |
| LinkedIn | Text post, Carousel outline, Pair with an image, Infographic brief |
| Instagram | Carousel outline, Pair with an image, Video script |
| Facebook | Text post, Pair with an image, Video script |
| TikTok, YouTube | Video script |
| Pinterest | Pair with an image, Infographic brief |
| Google Business | Text post, Pair with an image |
| Substack, Start Page | Text post |
| Unknown | Any of the formats below |

Their channels are the ones connected in Buffer, if it's available as a tool (busiest first), otherwise ask once where they post.

If the material supports more than one post (a different angle, or a channel or format that needs its own shape), prepare each one in full. Don't make the same post twice for two channels unless each is shaped for its channel. For each, write one plain sentence to them on why this channel and format fit this story. Put the strongest first, show the top three, and keep the rest behind "want to see more?". At most eight.

## 3. Prepare each post

They choose how much help they want. If they don't say, give the draft.

- **Draft from their words.** You're their editor, not their ghostwriter. Keep their words and phrases wherever they work: their vocabulary, images, opinions and the way they talk stay intact. You may tidy raw speech: add transitions, tighten a rambling sentence, fix grammar, pick a clearer verb, smooth the join between things said at different moments. Never add a claim, detail, number, feeling, metaphor, opinion, hook, call to action or hashtag they didn't give you, and never make it sound like marketing. Keep the names they used for things: if they said "my app" or "this project", write that. Never swap in a product, program or company name they mentioned elsewhere; a contest or program they built it for is not the name of what they built. Fix obvious speech-to-text mishearings when the context makes the right word clear. If an answer only makes sense next to its question, add the few words it needs to stand alone, and no more. It should read as them telling the story cleanly in one go.
- **Outline.** One beat per line, `[Open]`, `[Tension]`, `[Turn]`, `[Close]`, each followed by the phrase of theirs that carries it. You may drop filler and add the few small connecting words needed ("so", "and", "because"). For carousels, videos and infographics, add one bracketed line on the shape. No sentences of yours.

If they swore, leave the word out unless the line needs it, and tell them you softened it.

Square brackets hold structure and visual directions only, never words for the reader: "[Slide 2]", "[Image: the setup screen before and after]", "[On screen: the error message]". Keep each short. A short post may carry one image or visual direction of up to a couple of lines; otherwise directions stay well under half the post.

Formats:

- **Text post:** 60 to 150 words in short paragraphs. Open on their sentence that carries the tension or surprise as the hook, then right after it, in a line or two of their words, the context a stranger needs: what they were building or doing and who it's for. Never invent context they didn't give. Keep only the strongest four to six beats. End on their line about what it means or what changed.
- **Thread:** three to six parts separated by a blank line, each starting `1/`, `2/`…; each part stands on its own.
- **Carousel outline:** five to eight slides, each starting `[Slide N]`, then their words for that slide.
- **Pair with an image:** the post text, then one line `[Image: …]` saying what picture would carry it.
- **Video script:** 30 to 60 seconds spoken, in short lines, with `[On screen: …]` directions between.
- **Infographic brief:** four to six lines, each one point in their words, then one `[Visual: …]` line describing the layout.

## 4. Check it's theirs

Before you show a post, check it the way the app does. Leave aside the bracketed directions and look at the meaningful words (not "the", "and", "I"). At least two-thirds of them should come from their own sentences for a draft, and four in five for an outline. If a post falls short, rewrite it once using only their words for the parts that drifted. If it still falls short, give them their own sentences, cleaned of filler, and say you kept close to their exact words.

## 5. Questions to finish it

For each post, three questions that help them finish or adapt it, the way a sharp editor who knows them would. Each is under 15 words, grabs a specific detail they said and names it, and does a different job: find the scene, name the tension, pick up an unfinished thread, turn it toward their reader, or adapt it to another format. Questions only, never wording. For example (from a different interview; never copy these):

- "What does one line in the log look like, and what would that video's line have said?"
- "Proud and worried: which one wins most weeks?"
- "What's the simplest version a non-technical reader could copy tomorrow?"

## Long-form pieces

For one longer piece, find its one main idea, who it's for, the moment that carries it, the tension and the turn. Draft (or outline) 800 to 1,200 words when the material supports it, shorter when it doesn't, never padded. Open on their line that carries the tension, use three to five sections each starting `[Section: …]` on its own line, and end on their line about what they believe now. Offer two or three title options lifted from phrases they actually said, three finishing questions, and one short post per channel pointing to the piece (40 to 100 words, ending `[Link to the piece]`).

For both (a piece plus short posts), prepare the piece and its share posts first, then run steps 1 to 5 on the same conversation for standalone posts, leaving out anything the piece already covers.

## Saving

If Buffer is connected as a tool, offer to save each post to its channel's Drafts, never scheduled or published. For an outline, put the finishing questions under it. A long-form piece can't be a Buffer post: offer it as a Markdown file, or save it as a Buffer Idea. If Buffer refuses a draft (a plan limit, or a channel like Instagram that needs media), save it as an Idea instead and say so. Without Buffer, show everything ready to copy.

When they save or post something, add a line to `used.md`: date, story title, channel, and where it went.

## Asking for changes

If they ask for a change to one draft ("open with what I was building"), redo just that draft from the same interview. Don't start a new interview. Make the change they asked for and leave the rest; take anything new from their sentences or their request, and if it isn't anywhere in what they said, say what they could add instead of making it up. The same own-words rules apply.
